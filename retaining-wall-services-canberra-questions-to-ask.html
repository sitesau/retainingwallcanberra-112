Which site facts change the first question

Older inner-south gardens may have walls wrapped around established slopes, so the sharper question is service life, water paths and whether later landscaping added load. Weston Creek, Belconnen and Tuggeranong blocks can involve house benches cut into hillsides. Gungahlin and Molonglo Valley sites may inherit levels from subdivision earthworks, where fixed levels, neighbouring ground and documented constraints matter. Describe the change in levels and boundaries instead of relying on the postcode to explain risk.

How should I talk about materials without pre-choosing them

Treat concrete sleeper, segmental block and masonry, sandstone and natural stone, timber garden walls, gabion walls, drainage and excavation coordination, engineered approval-ready walls, and repairs or replacement as discussion pathways. Ask why a pathway fits: embedment and transitions for sleeper work; geometry, footing or reinforced-soil needs and footprint for block or masonry; bearing, appearance and access for stone or gabion; moisture exposure, fixings, drainage and future replacement for lower timber terraces. For repairs, insist the cause is named before scope, because movement, washout and cracking are symptoms.

What belongs in a written scope

A comparable scope records measured facts before any preferred product. It should state retained height, broader cut and fill, nearby loads, the drainage path, boundary conditions and access, and whether the job is new work, replacement, stabilisation or repair after movement or washout. Give drainage its own line item: collection zone, separator, subsoil drain, outlet, excavation access and spoil movement. A lower-detail reply can still be early-stage, provided it says what must be confirmed before pricing or specification.

Who checks approval and qualified roles

Keep building approval and development approval separate in your questions, because a wall can be a construction problem, a planning problem or both. You do not need to solve the pathway before the first conversation, but ask how the project will be checked against each one. Where loads are significant, boundaries are close, nearby structures are involved or ground conditions are unclear, a casual material suggestion is not enough. Ask which facts are still missing after inspection and whether design, certification and construction responsibilities are separated in writing.

  1. Document the whole level change. Record the maximum retained height, then photograph the full slope from both ends before taking close-ups.
  2. List loads and water. Note driveways, buildings, paving, fences and raised gardens, plus downpipes, overland flow, wet patches and likely outlets.
  3. Describe access and boundaries. Explain the route from street to wall, tight handling points and any neighbouring ground or fixed levels.
  4. Send one brief to every provider. Use the same photographs, height notes and access description so answers can be compared on water, loads, approvals and practicality.

Common questions

Do I need to know the approval pathway before requesting a quote? No. The useful step is to ask how the project will be checked against building approval and development approval, and which facts must be confirmed before that view is reliable.

Can I choose a wall type before the site is inspected? Treat concrete sleeper, block or masonry, stone, timber and gabion options as pathways for discussion. The source guide says suitability depends on geometry, embedment, footing or reinforced-soil needs, footprint, bearing, drainage, appearance and access.

What if the wall is only showing small cracks? Treat cracking, lean, decay, washout and persistent wetness as symptoms. Ask for the likely cause, the stabilisation or replacement path, and how water will be collected and discharged behind the new or repaired structure.
